# 📺 Prime Video — Turning Operational Complexity Into Self-Service Products

**Self-Service Product · Technical Product Management · Operational UX · Analytics · Product Strategy · Scale**

> **Turning repeated expert investigations into products that helped operations teams understand problems, prioritize exceptions, and act without waiting for a specialist.**

## At a Glance

**Role:** Senior BI Engineer → Analytics Manager  
**Scope:** Built self-service operational products and later led a cross-functional team of product managers, program managers, data scientists, data engineers, and developers supporting Prime Video's digital supply chain and expansion into live sports.  
**Users:** Internal operations teams responsible for getting content ready and available to Prime Video customers.  
**Product:** Self-service capabilities that brought fragmented operational context together around the questions users actually needed to answer.  
**Outcome:** Shifted recurring investigations away from specialist-dependent workflows toward reusable self-service capabilities, giving operations teams greater independence while allowing specialists to focus on problems requiring deeper judgment.

---

## The Problem

At Prime Video's scale, a seemingly simple customer expectation —

> **“This title should be available to watch.”**

— can depend on a complicated set of processes happening behind the scenes.

As the catalog and business grew, so did that operational complexity.

Teams responsible for getting content ready for customers regularly needed to answer questions such as:

- Is this title ready?
- If not, what's missing?
- Where does the problem appear to be?
- Does someone need to act?
- How urgent is it?

The problem wasn't a lack of data.

There was plenty of data.

The problem was that the information needed to make a decision was fragmented across different systems, workflows, and operational processes.

A user might know:

> **“This content isn't available when I expect it to be.”**

But getting from that observation to:

> **“I understand what's wrong and what needs to happen next.”**

could require significant investigation — often involving an analyst or specialist who knew where to look and how to assemble the context.

When every investigation depends on an expert, the expert becomes part of the workflow.

That can work at small scale.

It becomes a bottleneck as the business grows.

---

## Reframe the Problem as Self-Service

The obvious request might have been:

> **“Can you build us a dashboard?”**

But that wasn't really the problem.

A dashboard gives someone information.

A self-service product helps someone complete a job.

So I reframed the product question:

> **How do we give the people closest to an operational problem enough visibility and context to investigate it themselves?**

The job wasn't simply *view data*.

It was:

**Find the problem → understand its status → determine what needs attention → take the next action**

That distinction shaped the product.

---

## Start With the Investigation

A recurring operational question was why a title wasn't available when or where someone expected it to be.

From the user's perspective:

> **“Why isn't this live?”**

The existing workflow could look roughly like:

**Availability issue → Operations asks specialist → Specialist investigates → Context is assembled → Operations acts**

I worked on self-service reporting and operational products designed to move more of that investigation directly into the hands of operations teams.

The target workflow became:

**Availability issue → Operations investigates → Likely problem becomes visible → Operations acts**

The key product decision wasn't displaying more information.

It was identifying the information that helped someone answer:

**What is happening? → What appears to be wrong? → Does this require action? → What should I do next?**

That is what turned analytics into a product.

---

## Organize Around the Decision, Not the Systems

Internal tools naturally tend to mirror the technology underneath them.

One system owns one piece of information. Another owns something else. A third contains another part of the workflow.

But users don't care which system owns the data.

They care about completing their job.

So instead of designing around:

> **“Here is everything System A knows.”**

the useful product view became:

> **“Here is what you need to understand about this title.”**

That meant bringing the relevant context together around the user's investigation rather than exposing the organizational structure of the underlying systems.

Conceptually, the experience moved from:

**Portfolio view → Problem area → Individual title → Relevant context → Next action**

The product became an investigation path instead of a wall of metrics.

---

## Turn Repeated Questions Into Product Capabilities

One of the most important patterns I learned to recognize was repetition.

If an expert repeatedly gets asked variations of:

> **“What's happening with this title?”**

that recurring analytical request is often **product discovery in disguise**.

The opportunity isn't necessarily to help the expert answer faster.

It's to determine which parts of the investigation can become reusable product capabilities.

The workflow changes from:

**Question → Analyst → Investigation → Answer**

to:

**Question → Product → Investigation → Action**

That creates leverage on both sides.

Operations teams gain independence.

Specialists regain time for ambiguous problems that actually require specialist judgment.

---

## Design for Exceptions

At Prime Video's scale, asking a human to inspect everything wasn't realistic.

The product therefore needed to help users distinguish between:

**Things progressing normally**

and

**Things that may require intervention**

The useful question wasn't:

> **“Can I see every possible data point?”**

It was:

> **“What deserves my attention right now?”**

That shifted the experience from passive monitoring toward exception management.

Rather than making operators search through large amounts of information to find problems, the product could increasingly help surface where investigation was actually needed.

## From Analytics to Product Leadership

My role evolved significantly during my time at Prime Video.

I started as a **Senior BI Engineer**, doing hands-on analytical and product work: investigating ambiguous operational problems, working directly with users, defining requirements, and building self-service capabilities.

I was later promoted into **analytics management**.

My scope expanded to leading a cross-functional team that included:

- product managers
- program managers
- data scientists
- data engineers
- developers

The team supported Prime Video's digital supply chain and its first expansion into live sports.

That changed the question I needed to answer as a leader.

Early on, it was:

> **“Can I solve this problem?”**

As my scope grew, it became:

> **“Can I create the priorities, systems, and context that allow a team to solve many problems without me?”**

The same self-service principle we were applying to our products also applied to how I led the team.

The goal was to reduce dependence on individual experts — including myself.

---

## Measure Capability, Not Dashboard Usage

For internal products, usage alone isn't a particularly meaningful success metric.

A heavily used dashboard can still represent a terrible workflow.

What mattered more was whether the product changed what users were capable of doing.

I thought about success through questions like:

- Can users complete more investigations independently?
- Are fewer recurring questions being routed to specialists?
- Can teams identify issues earlier?
- Is time-to-understanding improving?
- Are operators focusing attention on the problems that actually require intervention?
- Is the product replacing manual work rather than simply adding another layer to it?

The goal was **increased decision-making capability**, not dashboard adoption.

That distinction became central to how I think about internal products.

---

## Using Data to Challenge the Original Question

Analytics was useful not only because it could answer operational questions.

It could reveal that we were asking the wrong question in the first place.

Sometimes:

> **“Why did this happen?”**

became:

> **“Why does a human need to investigate this every time?”**

And:

> **“Can we make this report faster?”**

became:

> **“Why does this process require a report at all?”**

I regularly used data to identify root causes, challenge assumptions, and turn ambiguous operational problems into clearer product priorities.

That remains the part of analytics work that interests me most:

> **Using data to decide what should change.**

---

## What Made This Hard

**Fragmented context.**  
The information required to understand a problem could exist across multiple systems and processes. The product needed to hide that complexity rather than reproduce it.

**Designing for different levels of expertise.**  
Internal users weren't necessarily analysts or engineers. Giving someone access to data wasn't the same thing as giving them the ability to use it.

**Scale.**  
As catalog and operational volume grew, manual investigation and specialist-dependent workflows became increasingly difficult to sustain.

**Separating signal from noise.**  
The product couldn't simply expose everything. It needed to help users identify which exceptions deserved human attention.

## My Role

I joined Prime Video as a **Senior BI Engineer** and was later promoted to **Analytics Manager**.

My work sat at the intersection of analytics, product development, and operations.

I worked directly on self-service products, operational analytics, root-cause analysis, discovery, requirements, prioritization, and cross-functional execution.

As my role expanded, I led product managers, program managers, data scientists, data engineers, and developers supporting Prime Video's digital supply chain and expansion into live sports.

Across both phases of my role, the underlying product challenge remained remarkably consistent:

> **How do we turn knowledge held by specialists into systems that allow many more people to make good decisions?**

---

## What I Learned

Prime Video fundamentally changed how I think about self-service products.

> **Self-service isn't primarily about giving users access to information. It's about moving capability closer to the person who needs it.**

A successful self-service product changes the organization from:

**“I need someone to answer this for me.”**

to:

**“I know how to figure this out.”**

And the best version goes one step further:

> **“The product already knows what deserves my attention.”**

That principle has followed me into every data product, internal tool, and operational system I've worked on since.
