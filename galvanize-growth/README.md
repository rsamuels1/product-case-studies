# 📈 Galvanize — Redesigning a Complex Customer Journey for Conversion

**Growth Product Management · Customer Research · Funnel Analytics · Experimentation · Information Architecture**

Galvanize had a complicated customer journey.

The company served different audiences across education, coworking, enterprise, events, and multiple physical campuses. The website had to help people understand what Galvanize offered, determine whether it was relevant to them, and move toward the right next step without forcing them to understand how the company was organized internally.

I worked on that as a growth product problem.

The question wasn’t just:

**“How should the website look?”**

It was:

**“How do we make it easier for different customers to find the information they need, understand the value of the product, and take the next meaningful action?”**

---

## The Problem

Galvanize’s customer journey started online but often continued through very different offline and sales experiences.

A prospective student might be:

- exploring a career change;
- comparing programs;
- evaluating cost and financial aid;
- trying to understand outcomes;
- looking for a campus;
- attending an event;
- talking to admissions;
- or deciding whether they were ready to apply.

Other customers were looking for coworking, enterprise training, hiring, or community programs.

That meant a single website had to support several very different journeys without becoming a reflection of Galvanize’s internal org chart.

The growth challenge was to reduce that complexity for the user while still creating clear paths toward conversion.

---

## What I Learned

### Customers needed answers in a predictable order

For education products, I structured the experience around the actual questions a prospective student was likely to have:

1. What is the course?
2. What outcome can I expect?
3. What is the format?
4. What will I learn?
5. Who will teach me?
6. What does it cost?
7. Is financial assistance available?
8. Where is it?
9. When does it start?
10. What if I’m not ready yet?
11. Where can I get more answers?
12. What have previous students achieved?
13. What career support is available?
14. How do I get started?

That structure was intentionally different from organizing pages around internal departments or program ownership.

### Different audiences used different language

The site needed to reflect the language users understood rather than forcing them into internal Galvanize terminology.

That affected:

- navigation;
- labels;
- page hierarchy;
- taxonomy;
- content organization;
- search;
- and calls to action.

The goal was to make the experience feel obvious to the customer even if the underlying business was complicated.

### Customer behavior could help segment the experience

The redesign used an audience-, task-, and topic-oriented information architecture.

Instead of assuming every visitor should see the same path, the site could let people identify themselves through their behavior and then surface more relevant information.

That created opportunities for better discovery, cross-selling, and more relevant content throughout the journey.

## The Product Strategy

### Design around user intent, not company structure

The site architecture was built around helping customers answer:

**Where am I?**

**What can I do here?**

**What matters to me?**

**What should I do next?**

That meant creating multiple ways to reach the same important information rather than assuming every customer would navigate the site the same way.

### Make conversion explicit

Calls to action were designed with hierarchy:

- primary CTAs were prominent;
- secondary CTAs were less dominant;
- upsell CTAs could appear based on user behavior.

The CTA and form structure also supported cleaner conversion measurement by routing users to the appropriate lead forms and completion pages.

The product and site structure were deliberately designed to support both conversion and cross-sell opportunities.

### Build the website as a reusable system

The redesign wasn’t intended to be a collection of manually maintained pages.

I proposed modular content for:

- courses;
- instructors;
- events;
- forms;
- hiring partners;
- student projects;
- and supporting content.

The goal was to let teams maintain information once and reuse it across the site, reducing future development work while keeping customer-facing information more consistent.

The strategy also explored using customer or email attributes to surface more relevant content dynamically.

## Customer Research & Validation

The redesign plan included continuous usability testing as new parts of the sitemap were released.

One technique was **card sorting** to test whether the labeling and navigation hierarchy matched the way users actually grouped information.

Research inputs included:

- subject-matter experts;
- instructors and content creators;
- external user testing;
- paid-acquisition learnings;
- SEO keyword research;
- and internal stakeholder discovery.

The point was not to treat the initial architecture as finished.

It was a hypothesis to validate and refine.

## Growth & Measurement

The website work sat inside a broader analytics and experimentation system I was building at Galvanize.

I owned or helped lead capabilities across:

- Google Analytics;
- Optimizely;
- SEO;
- paid acquisition;
- marketing automation;
- customer lifecycle measurement;
- data visualization;
- and the underlying data warehouse.

The analytics strategy was explicitly focused on finding **“leaky pipes”** in the business and identifying opportunities that were working against growth.

We were trying to connect the full lifecycle rather than optimize isolated marketing channels.

That meant understanding the customer from initial acquisition through downstream outcomes.

---

## Example: Connecting Offline Events to the Customer Funnel

One example of that approach was event measurement.

Galvanize hosted a large number of educational events, but event attendance alone didn’t tell us whether those programs were contributing to the business.

I connected event data to the education funnel so we could compare different event types against downstream applications and enrollments.

In one Q1 analysis:

- roughly **6% of all education-event attendees applied**;
- roughly **2% of attendees at evangelist events applied**;
- roughly **18% of info-session attendees applied**.

That changed the question from:

**“How many people attended?”**

to:

**“Which experiences actually move customers forward?”**

I also documented the gaps in the underlying data rather than overstating the analysis, with the goal of eventually turning it into a refreshable stakeholder dashboard as more data became available.

## Experimentation

Experimentation was part of the operating model, not an isolated analytics exercise.

I owned the A/B testing platform and designed experiments across new and existing experiences.

The process was:

**Observe behavior → identify friction → define a hypothesis → change the experience → measure the result**

That could mean testing:

- labels;
- navigation;
- page structure;
- CTAs;
- content hierarchy;
- or other parts of the customer journey.

The broader goal was to create a culture where product and growth decisions could be tested instead of debated indefinitely.

## What I’d Do Differently Today

I would make the experimentation framework even more explicit from the beginning.

The original work contained strong product thinking, customer research, instrumentation, and testing plans, but today I would formalize each major experience change around:

**Problem → evidence → hypothesis → experiment → success metric → decision**

I would also invest earlier in event-level behavioral instrumentation so qualitative research and funnel data could be evaluated together rather than in parallel.

And I would use faster prototyping and AI-assisted development to test more interaction and content hypotheses before committing engineering resources.

This project changed how I think about growth.

Growth isn’t just acquisition.

It’s what happens when you understand:

- who the customer is;
- what they are trying to accomplish;
- where they get confused or stop;
- what information they need next;
- and how you can remove enough friction for them to keep moving.
